# 1:1 Prep & Follow-Up

The 1:1 is where you find out a person is leaving — three months before they tell you, if you run it well, or on the day they resign, if you don't. This skill prepares the meeting, gives you the questions for the situation you're actually in, and makes sure what was agreed becomes a task instead of a good intention.

> Tool placeholders like `~~project tracker` mean whatever tool you've connected in that category. See [CONNECTORS.md](CONNECTORS.md).

## What I need from you

**Option A — Connected tracker.** "Prep my 1:1 with Dana." I'll pull her open and overdue work first.

**Option B — Tell me.** Their name, how long they've been on the team, when you last met, and anything on your mind about them. Two lines is enough.

**Option C — After the fact.** Paste your rough notes or the meeting transcript and say "log my 1:1 with Dana."

## Step 1 — Decide which mode you're in, before pulling anything

Three different jobs hide behind the same words. Getting this wrong wastes the session — building a full agenda when someone just asked a passing question is as bad as answering casually when they're about to walk into a hard conversation.

| Mode | Signal | What it must produce |
|---|---|---|
| **1. General inquiry** | "how's Dana doing", "what's Dana working on" — no meeting mentioned | A direct answer. No agenda, no writes. |
| **2. Meeting prep** | "prep my 1:1", "1:1 agenda", "what should I raise with" | An agenda, delivered, nothing written anywhere yet. |
| **3. Post-meeting follow-up** | "log my 1:1", "follow up on my meeting", past tense, or pasted notes | A summary they confirm, then proposed action items. |

If it's genuinely ambiguous, ask one question rather than guess. The expensive wrong guess is treating a *prep* request as a *follow-up* and creating tasks for a meeting that hasn't happened yet.

## Step 2 — Mode 1: General inquiry

Pull their open and overdue tasks from `~~project tracker`, plus any comment trail you've left on their work. Answer in plain sentences. No template, no task creation, no advice they didn't ask for.

Do not pull or ask for engagement scores, attrition risk, or formal review data — this skill is for the day-to-day relationship, not a review process. If a real performance concern is forming, that belongs in Mode 2 with the performance-concern agenda, not in a data lookup.

## Step 3 — Mode 2: Meeting prep

**First, check the last 1:1's action items.** If a previous summary or task comment exists, look at whether those items were actually completed *before* building the new agenda. This is the single thing that separates a 1:1 that builds trust from one that feels like a status meeting. Open the agenda with it.

**Then pull their current workload** from `~~project tracker` — open tasks, anything overdue and by how many days — so you're not walking in blind on work status. Knowing their load is not the same as auditing it; the point is context, not a report card.

**Then pick the agenda type:**

| Situation | Template |
|---|---|
| Normal week, nothing unusual | Regular check-in |
| Monthly/quarterly, or they raised growth | Career conversation |
| Something specific has gone wrong twice or more | Performance concern |
| On the team under 90 days | New team member |

Full templates, question bank, self-check, red flags and anti-patterns are in the **1:1 Framework Reference** below — use them verbatim, don't paraphrase them into vaguer versions.

If feedback is part of this 1:1, script it with SBI-I from the **Radical Candor Reference** before the meeting. Feedback you improvise in the room comes out either too soft to land or harder than you meant.

**Deliver the agenda. Write nothing yet.**

### Output — the agenda

Use this structure literally.

```
1:1 PREP — [Name] — [date]

Last 1:1: [date] — [n] action items, [n] done, [n] still open
  → [open item + how many days old]        (only if any are open)

Workload: [n] open tasks · [n] overdue — [worst one + days late]

Agenda type: [regular check-in / career / performance concern / new team member]

## 1:1 with [Name] — [date]

### Their topics (5-15 min)
- [let them lead — do not fill this in for them]

### How are you doing? (5 min)
- Energy level / workload
- Blockers or frustrations
- Anything I should know?

### Work check-in (5-10 min)
- What's going well? What's challenging?
- Do you have what you need?

### To raise (only if you have something)
- [the item] — say it like this: "[the actual sentence, SBI form]"

### Growth / development (5 min)
- [one question from the bank matched to their situation]

### My topics (5 min)
- [your items — keep brief, these go last on purpose]

### Action items
- [ ]

⚠️ Before you walk in:
- [Feedback you've been avoiding for [n] weeks — say it this time or drop it.]
- [You cancelled the last one. Acknowledge it in the first minute.]
- [You owe them an action item from last time. Do it before the meeting, not after.]
```

## Step 4 — Mode 3: Post-meeting follow-up

**Find the meeting content, in this order:**

1. `~~meeting notes` — pull the transcript or summary. **Sanity-check it before trusting it.** Noisy rooms and code-switched conversations transcribe as confident nonsense. If it reads incoherently, say so and ask the manager to confirm rather than building a summary from garbage — a wrong summary logged as fact is worse than no summary.
2. `~~calendar` — if the date is vague ("my 1:1 last week"), find the meeting first, then go back to step 1.
3. Ask: *"What did you discuss?"* A few bullets is enough. Don't demand a transcript.

**Propose the summary, don't assert it.** *"Here's what I'd capture — anything to add or fix?"* You were not in the room. The manager was.

**Propose action items. Never decide and create them.** For each: title, owner, due date, priority — then wait for an explicit yes before writing anything to `~~project tracker`. Then report what was actually created, not what you intended to create.

### Output — the follow-up

```
1:1 SUMMARY — [Name] — [date]

What we covered:
- [point]
- [point]

What they raised:
- [their topics, in their words where possible]

Decisions / agreements:
- [what was agreed]

Proposed action items — say yes and I'll create them:
1. [title] — owner: [name] — due: [real date] — priority: [urgent/high/med/low]
2. [title] — owner: [name] — due: [real date] — priority: [...]

Summary comment to post: [1-3 plain sentences]   ← post this even with zero action items

⚠️ For you only:
- [They said "everything's fine" three 1:1s in a row. That's a signal, not an answer.]
- [Second time they raised the same blocker. It's now yours, not theirs.]
- [You committed to something. It's item 2 and it's assigned to you.]
```

## The rules that make this work

**Every action item gets a real due date.** Ask what was agreed. If nothing was agreed, default by priority: urgent or high → today, medium → tomorrow, low → this Friday. Not "next week." A date without a day is a wish, and it will still be open at the next 1:1, which is exactly how 1:1s lose their credibility.

**Assign to the right person.** Half of what's agreed in a 1:1 is the manager's job, not the report's — unblocking, chasing another team, getting an approval. Default-assigning everything to the report is how a supportive conversation quietly becomes a to-do list handed down. Set the owner explicitly, every time.

**Propose before creating.** Nothing gets written to `~~project tracker` — task, comment, status change — until the manager says yes. What sounded like a commitment in the room was often thinking out loud, and a task created from thinking out loud makes the next 1:1 defensive.

**Always post a summary comment, even when there were zero action items.** A "just checking in" 1:1 with nothing to do still deserves a trail. That comment *is* the durable 1:1 history — in six months, when you're writing a promotion case or a difficult conversation is coming, that chain of small comments is the only evidence that exists. Skipping it because "nothing happened" is how a year of 1:1s ends up unprovable.

**Start every prep by checking whether the last one's items got done.** If your own items are undone, say that out loud before you ask about theirs. A manager who chases their report's commitments and quietly drops their own teaches the report that the meeting is theatre.

**Their topics go first, yours go last.** If you run out of time, the thing that gets cut should be your update, not their concern. Reversing that order once is forgivable; doing it every week is what turns a 1:1 into a status meeting.

## What NOT to do

- **Never pull or ask for engagement scores, attrition risk, or formal review data.** This skill is for the day-to-day relationship, not a review process. Walking into a 1:1 holding a risk score changes how you listen, and they will feel it.
- Don't guess at project IDs, workspace names, or profile details — ask, or check the app.
- Don't bulk-create tasks for a whole team without each person's own agreement from their own 1:1.
- Don't decide follow-up action items and create them without proposing them first, even when the conversation obviously implies them.
- Don't fill in "their topics" for them. An empty section is data.

## 1:1 Framework Reference (full)

> Adapted from the open-source `jkbennemann/leadership-skills` project (Apache 2.0 License).

### Why 1:1s matter

1:1s are the most important meeting a manager has. They are:
- **Their meeting, not yours** — the direct report sets the agenda
- **About them, not status** — save project updates for standups
- **Relationship-building time** — trust compounds over time
- **An early warning system** — problems surface here first

### The four principles

1. **Consistency over intensity** — weekly or biweekly, same time, never cancel. 30 minutes minimum, 45-60 ideal. Rescheduling is okay; canceling erodes trust.
2. **They own the agenda** — ask them to bring topics; your items come second. If they have nothing, dig deeper — that's a signal, not a free 30 minutes.
3. **Listen more than talk** — aim for a 70/30 ratio (them/you). Ask follow-up questions. Silence is okay; let them think.
4. **Document and follow through** — take notes (or have them take notes), track action items, and start the next 1:1 with the previous action items.

### Agenda templates by type

**Type 1 — Regular check-in (weekly/biweekly).** Purpose: maintain connection, surface small issues before they grow.

```
## 1:1 with [Name] — [Date]

### Their Topics (5-15 min)
- [Let them lead]

### How are you doing? (5 min)
- Energy level / workload
- Blockers or frustrations
- Anything I should know?

### Work Check-in (5-10 min)
- What's going well?
- What's challenging?
- Do you have what you need?

### Growth/Development (5 min)
- Any learning opportunities this week?
- Skills you want to develop?

### My Topics (5 min)
- [Your items — keep brief]

### Action Items
- [ ] ...
```

**Type 2 — Career conversation (monthly/quarterly).** Purpose: discuss long-term growth, aspirations, development.

```
## Career Conversation with [Name] — [Date]

### Reflection
- What's energized you most in the last quarter?
- What's drained you?
- What accomplishment are you most proud of?

### Current Role
- What parts of your job do you love?
- What would you change if you could?
- Are you learning and growing?

### Future Direction
- Where do you see yourself in 1-2 years?
- What skills do you want to develop?
- What experiences do you want to have?

### Support Needed
- How can I help you get there?
- What opportunities should I look for?
- What's in your way?

### Action Items
- [ ] ...
```

**Type 3 — Performance concern.** Purpose: address issues early, create clarity on expectations. Never open with "don't take this personally."

```
## 1:1 with [Name] — [Date] — Performance Discussion

### Opening (be direct)
"I want to talk about something important. I care about your
success here, which is why I'm raising this directly."

### The Issue (SBI format)
- Situation: [When/where]
- Behavior: [What specifically happened]
- Impact: [The effect on team/work/customers]

### Their Perspective
- "What's your view on this?"
- "What's getting in the way?"
- "Is there context I'm missing?"

### Expectations Going Forward
- [Specific, measurable changes needed]
- [Timeline for improvement]
- [Support you'll provide]

### Check Understanding
- "Can you summarize what we agreed?"
- "What questions do you have?"

### Action Items
- [ ] [Specific behavior change]
- [ ] [Follow-up date]
- [ ] [Support/resources to provide]
```

**Type 4 — New team member (first 90 days).** Purpose: accelerate onboarding, build the relationship, identify gaps.

*Weeks 1-2 — settling in:*
```
- How are you settling in?
- What's confusing or unclear?
- Do you have everything you need (access, equipment, etc.)?
- Who have you met? Who should you meet?
- What questions do you have about the team/company?
```

*Weeks 3-4 — surprises and quick wins:*
```
- What's surprised you (good or bad)?
- What's different from your previous role?
- Where do you see quick wins?
- What processes seem inefficient?
- How's the pace — too fast, too slow?
```

*Months 2-3 — confidence gaps and support:*
```
- Where do you feel confident?
- Where do you still feel uncertain?
- What training or support do you need?
- How's your relationship with the team?
- What would make you more effective?
```

**Bonus — returning from leave or a difficult period.** Welcome back, how are you *really* doing? What do you need from me right now? What pace feels right for ramping back up? What should I know but not share with others? How can I protect your time and energy? What's the best way to check in on you?

### Question bank by situation

**When they seem disengaged**
- "You've seemed quieter lately. What's on your mind?"
- "On a scale of 1-10, how excited are you about your work right now?"
- "What would need to change for this to be a 10?"
- "Are you getting to do what you do best every day?"
- "Is there something outside of work affecting you?"

**When they're frustrated**
- "Tell me more about that frustration."
- "What's the root cause?"
- "What have you tried so far?"
- "What would need to change?"
- "How can I help — do you want me to fix it, or just listen?"

**When they want a promotion**
- "What does that next level look like to you?"
- "What gaps do you see between where you are and where you want to be?"
- "What evidence would show you're ready?"
- "What can you start doing now at that level?"
- "How can I help you get there?"

**When they're overloaded**
- "Walk me through everything on your plate right now."
- "What would you take off if you could?"
- "What's urgent vs. important?"
- "What can I take off your plate or delegate elsewhere?"
- "Are you comfortable pushing back when asked to do more?"

**When there's team conflict**
- "Help me understand what's happening with [person]."
- "What's your role in this dynamic?"
- "What have you tried to resolve it?"
- "What outcome would you like to see?"
- "Would it help if I facilitated a conversation?"

**When they're a top performer**
- "What's keeping you here?"
- "What might make you leave?"
- "Are you being challenged enough?"
- "What's the most interesting problem you'd want to work on?"
- "How can I help you have more impact?"

**When they're quiet or give one-word answers**
- "If you had a magic wand, what would you change?"
- "What's the one thing you wish I understood better?"
- "What feedback do you have for me?"
- "What's something you've wanted to say but haven't?"
- Try a walking 1:1 or an informal coffee chat instead of a desk meeting.

**Every 1:1 — pick 1-2 to keep it flowing.** Opening: "What's top of mind for you?" / "What would make this a great week?" Middle: "What's your biggest challenge right now?" / "What do you need from me?" Closing: "Is there anything we didn't cover?" / "What's one thing I can do to support you this week?"

**Getting feedback on yourself (quarterly, not every 1:1).** "What's one thing I could do differently to support you better?" / "What am I not seeing that I should be aware of?" / "If you were in my shoes, what would you do differently?" / "What should I start, stop, or keep doing?" / "On a scale of 1-10, how well am I supporting you — and what would make it higher?"

### Manager self-check (before each 1:1)

| ✓ | Question |
|---|---|
| ☐ | Did I review notes from last 1:1? |
| ☐ | Did I follow through on my action items? |
| ☐ | Do I know what they're working on? |
| ☐ | Is there feedback I've been avoiding? |
| ☐ | Have I thought about their career lately? |
| ☐ | Am I in the right headspace to listen? |

### Red flags to watch for

| Signal | What it might mean |
|---|---|
| Always says "everything's fine" | Doesn't feel safe being honest |
| Never brings topics | Disengaged, or doesn't see the value |
| Frequently reschedules | Avoiding something |
| Only talks about tasks | Keeping you at a distance |
| Sudden mood or behavior change | Personal issue, or job searching |
| Stops asking questions | Checked out |
| Complains about the same thing repeatedly | Feels unheard |

### Anti-patterns to avoid

❌ **Status meeting** — "give me an update on your projects"
❌ **Your meeting** — you talk the whole time
❌ **Skipping regularly** — "we'll catch up next week"
❌ **No notes** — forgetting what you discussed
❌ **No follow-through** — not doing what you said you'd do
❌ **Only when there's a problem** — makes 1:1s feel like trouble
❌ **Multitasking** — checking chat or email during the meeting
❌ **Rushing** — ending early when they clearly have more

### Running it, and after

*First 2 minutes:* warm up, then "what would you like to talk about today?" *Their agenda (first half):* listen, ask follow-ups, take notes, don't jump straight to solutions. *Your items (second half):* brief, most important only, leave time for their reaction. *Last 5 minutes:* summarize, confirm who does what by when, "anything else before we wrap?"

*After:* document within 24 hours, do your own action items promptly, check in before the next 1:1 if something urgent came up, and reflect on what you learned.

*The best 1:1s feel like conversations, not meetings.*

## Radical Candor / Feedback Reference (full)

> Adapted from the open-source `jkbennemann/leadership-skills` project (Apache 2.0 License). Based on *Radical Candor* by Kim Scott.

### The four quadrants

Radical Candor sits at the intersection of two dimensions: how much you **care personally** and how much you **challenge directly**.

```
                    CARE PERSONALLY
                          ↑
     Ruinous              |            Radical
     Empathy              |            Candor
    "Nice but             |          "Kind and
     unhelpful"           |           clear"
                          |
   ←----------------------+----------------------→ CHALLENGE DIRECTLY
                          |
     Manipulative         |            Obnoxious
     Insincerity          |            Aggression
    "Passive              |          "Brutal
     aggressive"          |           honesty"
                          ↓
```

| Quadrant | Care | Challenge | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Radical Candor** | High | High | Growth and trust |
| **Ruinous Empathy** | High | Low | Stagnation, surprises at reviews |
| **Obnoxious Aggression** | Low | High | Fear, defensiveness |
| **Manipulative Insincerity** | Low | Low | Politics, backstabbing |

### Core principles

1. **Be humble** — you might be wrong. State your perspective, not absolute truth. Say "I noticed...", never "you always...".
2. **Be helpful** — the goal is their success, not your venting. Focus on behaviors they can change, and offer support alongside the criticism.
3. **Be immediate** — give feedback **within 2-3 days** of the event, while context is fresh for both of you. Small corrections prevent big problems; saved-up feedback arrives as an ambush.
4. **Be in person (or at least synchronous)** — praise in public, criticize in private. Allow dialogue and questions. Read the reaction and adjust.
5. **Be specific** — describe the situation, the observed behavior, and the impact it had.

### Feedback formula — SBI-I

- **S — Situation.** When and where did this happen? *"In yesterday's sprint review..."*
- **B — Behavior.** What specific, observable action occurred? *"...you interrupted Maria three times while she was presenting..."*
- **I — Impact.** What was the effect on you, the team, or the work? *"...which made it hard for her to finish her points, and I noticed the team seemed uncomfortable."*
- **I — Intention.** Show you care and invite dialogue. *"I know you're passionate about this project, and I want to help you contribute effectively. Can we talk about what was going on for you?"*

### Constructive feedback template

```
I care about your growth, so I want to share some feedback.

[Situation]: In [specific context]...
[Behavior]: I observed that [specific behavior]...
[Impact]: The effect was [concrete impact]...

I believe you can [positive framing of change].
What's your perspective on this? How can I support you?
```

For praise, use the same three parts and end with why it matters: *"I really appreciate this because [reason]. Keep doing this — it makes a real difference."*

### Example — a missed deadline

**Ruinous Empathy (avoid):** *"Don't worry about the deadline, I know you're busy."*

**Radical Candor:** *"I noticed the API migration is a week behind schedule. I know you've had a lot on your plate with the production incidents. I'm concerned because this blocks the frontend team. What's getting in the way, and how can I help you get back on track?"*

Same care, opposite outcome. The first one feels kind in the room and costs them the chance to fix it.

### Red flags to avoid

❌ Starting with "don't take this personally..."
❌ Using "but" after praise — it negates the praise
❌ Feedback sandwiches (praise-criticism-praise)
❌ Saying "everyone thinks..." — speak for yourself
❌ Bringing up old issues alongside new ones
❌ Giving feedback while you're angry
❌ Making it about personality instead of behavior
❌ Not offering to help or follow up

### Green flags to include

✅ "I" statements about your own observations
✅ Specific, recent examples
✅ Asking for their perspective
✅ Offering concrete support
✅ Scheduling a follow-up
✅ Acknowledging what they do well
✅ Expressing confidence in their ability to improve

### Receiving feedback yourself

1. **Listen fully** — don't interrupt or defend.
2. **Thank them** — "thank you for telling me this."
3. **Clarify** — "can you give me a specific example?"
4. **Reflect** — "let me think about this."
5. **Follow up** — circle back once you've processed it.

Before giving any feedback, check: do I care, am I being direct, am I being specific, am I being timely, am I being humble, and am I offering help?

## Worked example

**Input:** *"prep my 1:1 with dana tomorrow, havent met in 3 weeks, she seemed off on the client call and shes been asking about the senior title"*

**Output:**

```
1:1 PREP — Dana — Aug 13

Last 1:1: Jul 23 — 2 action items, 1 done, 1 still open
  → "Get Dana access to the analytics account" — YOUR item, 21 days old

Workload: 4 open tasks · 1 overdue — "Client renewal notes", 8 days late, no update

Agenda type: Regular check-in, with a career thread

## 1:1 with Dana — Aug 13

### Their topics (5-15 min)
- Let her lead. She may open with the title question — let her.

### How are you doing? (5 min)
- Energy, workload, blockers
- "You've seemed quieter lately. What's on your mind?"

### Work check-in (5-10 min)
- Renewal notes are 8 days late with no update — ask, don't accuse:
  "I noticed the renewal notes were due last week and I haven't seen an
   update. What's getting in the way, and how can I help?"

### Career thread (5 min) — she raised the senior title
- "What does that next level look like to you?"
- "What gaps do you see between where you are and where you want to be?"
- "What evidence would show you're ready?"

### My topics (5 min)

### Action items
- [ ]

⚠️ Before you walk in:
- Three weeks between 1:1s. Say so and reset the cadence — weekly or
  biweekly, same slot. The gap is why the renewal notes went unnoticed.
- Your analytics-access item is 21 days old. Do it TODAY, before the
  meeting. Asking about her overdue task while yours is open costs you
  the right to ask.
- She asked about the title and you have not answered. If the answer is
  "not this cycle", say that tomorrow. Silence reads as no, delivered
  badly — that's Ruinous Empathy, and it ends in a resignation.
```

## Tips

1. **The gap between 1:1s is the real metric, not the meeting itself.** Three weeks apart isn't a busy schedule, it's a signal you sent them. If you can only protect 30 minutes, protect it weekly rather than doing 60 minutes when you happen to have room.
2. **"Everything's fine" three times in a row is a red flag, not a green one.** Nobody's job is fine for six straight weeks. It means they've decided the honest answer isn't worth the risk — go read the disengaged question set.
3. **If you're dreading the 1:1, that IS the agenda.** Whatever you're dreading is the thing that needs saying, and it gets more expensive every week you carry it. Script it with SBI-I and open with it.
4. **The comment you post after a nothing-happened 1:1 is the one that pays off.** A year of small notes is what lets you write an honest promotion case or a fair performance conversation. Memory won't do it, and you'll need the record exactly when it's too late to build one.
5. **Your best performer needs the most 1:1 time, not the least.** They cause no problems, so they get skipped — and they're the one with the most options. "What's keeping you here?" is a question you want to ask before you find out the hard way.
