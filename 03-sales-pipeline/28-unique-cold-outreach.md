# Unique Cold Outreach

A merge-tag email is structurally identical on every send. Same sentence shapes, same length, same links, same rhythm — only the name changes. That sameness is exactly what spam filters score and what recipients learn to recognise in half a second. This skill produces real per-contact variation, grounded in what you actually know about each person, not a swapped first name.

> Tool placeholders like `~~CRM` mean whatever tool you've connected in that category. See [CONNECTORS.md](CONNECTORS.md).

## How it works

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STANDALONE (always works)                                    │
│  ✓ Paste a list: name, title, company, what they do           │
│  ✓ Unique draft per contact, flagged risks, ready to review   │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  SUPERCHARGED (when you connect your tools)                   │
│  + ~~CRM: prior touches, tags, niche, reply history           │
│  + ~~email: whether this person already wrote back            │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## What I need from you

1. **The list** — ideally the output of [`27 ICP List Build`](27-icp-list-build.md). At minimum: name, title, company, and one true thing about what the company does.
2. **The core message** — what you actually want to say, the offer, the link, the ask.
3. **An approved subject line pool** — 2 to 4 options to rotate. Never invented fresh mid-batch. See the rule below; this one has burned people.

## Step 1 — Check history before drafting anything

For every contact, before a word is written:

| What the history shows | What to draft |
|---|---|
| Never contacted | Touch 1. Standard cold open. |
| Contacted, no reply | A follow-up. Reference the earlier note in half a sentence, with no guilt. Never re-send the same content. |
| **Contacted, and they replied** | **Nothing.** Pull them out of the batch and flag them. This person needs a human reply, not another campaign email. |

That third row is the one that costs real money. A live lead swallowed by a batch process is worse than never having run the batch.

## Step 2 — Build variation on three independent axes

Real uniqueness comes from mixing three things per contact, not from a synonym pass:

**1. A specific reference to what they actually do.** Pull it from their business name, their tags, their site — "packaging and branding", "a web shop that mostly does ecommerce", "a studio doing brand work for restaurants". This is the highest-leverage line in the email. It proves a human looked, even when a human did not write every word.

**2. The subject line.** Rotate through the approved pool. Never the same subject twice in one day's batch if you can avoid it.

**3. The shape of the opening sentence.** Rotate between a direct question, a one-line observation about their work, and a plain statement of why you are writing. Do not use the same shape twice in a row.

Three axes, mixed, gives you a batch where no two emails share a fingerprint — while every email still says the same thing.

## Step 3 — Keep the ask identical

Uniqueness applies to the wrapper, not the offer.

The actual ask — what you want them to do, the link, the value proposition — stays **consistent across the whole batch**. Varying the ask makes the campaign incoherent and makes the results impossible to read: when three versions of the offer go out and two people reply, you have learned nothing.

Vary how you arrive at the ask. Never the ask.

## Output

For each contact:

```
To: [email]
Subject: [one from the approved pool, rotated]

Hey [first name],

[1-2 sentences: the specific true reference + the core message]

[The ask, in this contact's variant phrasing]

[Sign-off]
```

Then, before anything goes out:

```
⚠️ Before you send

Pulled from the batch: [contacts who already replied — they need a human answer]
Thin personalization: [contacts where the specific detail was weak or generic]
Going out: [count] · No repeated subject + opening combination in this batch
```

## The rules that make this work

**The specific detail must be true.** Never invent what a company does. Pull it from real data. "Saw you do branding work" sent to a company that does not do branding is worse than generic copy — it proves the personalization is fake, which is a much more expensive impression than being generic.

**Never let a subject line drift without approval.** This is the rule that exists because of a real failure: an approved subject was quietly changed to something more "engaging" mid-send by an automated workflow, and nobody noticed until the batch was out. The fix is structural, not a matter of taste. Pull subjects only from the pre-approved pool. Never generate a new one mid-batch. If you want a new one, go back and get a yes.

**A reply means stop drafting and start replying.** Treating a reply as just another row in the list is how a real lead gets lost inside a process.

**A follow-up that pretends the first email never happened** reads as either spam or forgetfulness. Acknowledge it briefly, then move on.

**If you cannot find one true specific thing about a contact, do not invent one to compensate.** Flag it back to the list step. That is a list problem wearing a copywriting costume.

## Worked example

Two contacts from the same batch, same offer, same ask.

**Rafael, a studio doing branding and web work.** No prior contact.
> Subject: *quick one for the studio* (pool option 2)
> Opening shape: direct question
> Reference: "branding work"

**Scott, a web design shop.** No prior contact.
> Subject: *mostly out of curiosity* (pool option 3 — different from Rafael's)
> Opening shape: observation
> Reference: "web design shop", phrased differently from Rafael's line even though the underlying tag was similar

Both close with the identical ask: look at the link, reply with which one they would use. Only the wrapper differs.

A third contact, Dana, had replied to a note six weeks earlier. She was pulled out of the batch entirely and flagged for a personal reply.

## Tips

1. **Twenty genuinely unique emails is a completely different job from two hundred.** This skill is built for tens. At hundreds, per-contact grounding stops being achievable and you need real segmentation instead — say so rather than quietly producing thin variation at scale.
2. **Read three of the drafts side by side before sending.** If they feel interchangeable to you, they will to the recipient.
3. **The reply is the whole point.** If a batch goes out and nobody is watching the inbox for two days, the personalization was wasted effort.
4. **Keep the subject pool short.** Four good subjects rotated beats twelve mediocre ones, and a short pool is easy to approve properly.
5. **Send in small waves.** Twenty today tells you whether the message works before you spend the other eighty on a message that does not.
