# Owner Email Rules

Most agency owners have no email problem. They have an email *consistency* problem: one email sounds like them, the next sounds like a marketing automation, and the recipient can tell which is which. This file is the single place the rules live, so every email leaves the same way.

Read it before writing. Not from memory.

> Tool placeholders like `~~CRM` mean whatever tool you've connected in that category. See [CONNECTORS.md](../CONNECTORS.md).

## What I need from you, once

Fill these in the first time and keep them at the top of your copy of this file:

- Your exact signature block, and whether it includes a phone number
- Your meeting link, if you use one
- Your CRM logging address, if your CRM has a BCC address
- Any words you personally never use

## 1. Voice

Write plain English. Not simplified — plain.

- Short sentences. One idea each.
- Simple words. If a simpler word exists, use it.
- No warm-up. The first sentence is the actual point.
- Conversational but professional. A smart colleague texting you, not a consultant writing a report.
- Paragraphs of 1 to 3 sentences. One-sentence paragraphs are fine. White space is good.
- Contractions. It's, we've, I'd, they're.

**Do not sand your own writing smooth.** The version an AI hands back is always slightly more formal and slightly more even than how you actually type, and that evenness is what reads as machine-written. If you naturally drop an apostrophe or start a sentence lowercase on your phone, that is not an error to be corrected out. Keep the email sounding like the person, not the assistant. This applies to email only — never to a post, a blog, a proposal, or anything public and permanent.

## 2. The value formula — Share, Advise, Invite, Ask

Every email carries **one** value item, worked into the prose. Not bolted on. Not stacked.

| Move | What it is | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Share** | Give something real with no strings. A result, a number, a thing you built, a story from another client. | Warm contact, no active ask. Check-ins, anniversary notes. |
| **Advise** | A specific observation about *their* situation. Requires you actually know something. | You have real context: their role, their industry, something they said. |
| **Invite** | Offer access. A live look, an early demo, a seat at something. | There is genuine interest, or you have something new to show. |
| **Ask** | Request one thing. A call, an intro, an opinion. | The relationship is warm enough to spend the goodwill. |

**One per email.** An email that Shares and Advises and Invites and Asks is a pitch, and reads like one.

**Ask is the most expensive.** Never open with it on a cold or long-dormant contact.

**Never label it.** The recipient should not be able to see the formula.

**Re-engagement and thank-you notes should usually be Share only**, with the ask demoted to "reply if you want to catch up." A no-ask email gets replies precisely *because* it has no ask.

## 3. Deliver, do not promise

This is the strongest pattern in good owner email and the one most often missed.

The email carries the thing. It does not announce that the thing is coming.

Before drafting, ask: **what is the artifact in this email?** A link, a login, a document, a number, a decision. If the honest answer is "nothing yet, I'm telling them something is on its way," the email is not ready to write.

> "Good to talk just now. I set up everything we went through, so its all ready." — carrying the actual login

> "You asked for something on the security side for the CEO conversation. Here it is." — carrying the actual deck

Against a draft that had to be rewritten:

> "I'm putting together a short list of the live ones so you can see what's real."

That asks the recipient to wait. Nothing to open, nothing to react to, nothing to reply to except yes or no.

**If the deliverable genuinely is not ready, build it and send afterwards.** Never send a placeholder.

## 4. Give them one thing they did not know

The best owner emails carry a single line of real expertise the recipient could not have supplied themselves. It is what makes the email worth opening instead of worth filing.

> "One thing worth knowing before you present it. Theres no such thing as HIPAA certification, no agency issues one to anybody. So the deck shows what we actually do, the BAA, the encryption, the access control, the audit log, instead of a compliance badge. If their IT person asks to see a certificate, thats the answer and its already on the slide."

That paragraph is the email. Everything else is delivery.

**Never feed someone their own insight back to them.** If they said it on the call, it is theirs. Quoting it back is fine to build on or to disagree with. Restating it as news reads as if nobody was listening.

**Use their words for their things.** Calling someone's carefully customised tool "the Pi thing" is dismissive, even when it is shorter.

**Concrete over abstract, always.** "9 slides." "40+ hours a week." "One-time link, so theres no password to remember." Real numbers and real nouns. Never rounded up, never invented. If the number came from a transcript, use the number in the transcript.

## 5. Banned words and phrases

### Words

Elevate · Leverage · Resonate · Unleash · Unlock · Empower · Navigate · Transformative · Innovative · Groundbreaking · Cutting-edge · Revolutionary · Synergy · Paradigm · Ecosystem · Holistic · Robust · Scalable · Streamline · Optimize · Seamless · Reimagine · Spearhead · Foster · Testament · Delve · Enrich · Beacon · Adhere · Realm · Furthermore · Profound · Supercharge · Evolve · Pivotal

### Phrases

"I hope this finds you well" · "I wanted to reach out" · "Please don't hesitate" · "Looking forward to connecting" · "In today's fast-paced world" · "It goes without saying" · "It's important to note" · "Excited to share" · "I am writing to" · "game-changer" · "best-in-class" · "value-add" · "thought leadership" · "deep dive" · "In summary" · "At the end of the day" · "Moving forward" · "Circle back" · "Touch base" · "Move the needle"

### Formatting

No em dashes. No bullet points or numbered lists. No bold, italics, or headers.

Prose only. If the content genuinely needs a list, the email is too long. Cut it.

## 6. Length, subject, and close

**Under 150 words.** Good post-call emails run 120 to 160.

**Subject lines** are lowercase-ish, human, and specific. Name what is inside the email, not the relationship:

> `your portal login and the shared drive` · `security deck for your CEO conversation` · `been too long` · `22 years today` · `one question for an agency owner`

Never title case. Never a marketing subject. "22 years today", not "Celebrating Our 22nd Anniversary".

**Close open, not narrow.**

> "Have a look and tell me what you think." · "Have a look and tell me what youd change."

A narrow question — "which one should we start with?" — gets a one-word answer and ends the conversation. Asking for their reaction to the thing you just handed over gets a real reply, and it respects that they know something you do not.

**One action at the close. Never two.**

## 7. Reply or new email — decide this first

Getting this wrong is the single most damaging email mistake. A cold intro sent to someone you spoke to last week reads like it came from a bot with no memory.

**Search wide first.** Run all of these against `~~email`, with no date cap:

```
from:{their address} OR to:{their address}
"{first name} {last name}"
"{company name}"
{any alternate addresses on the CRM record}
```

Watch for CRM records holding several comma-separated addresses in one field. Exact-match search silently misses those. Search each address separately.

**Then read the most recent thread properly** — the body, not the snippet. Their own phrasing is the best opener available and the snippet will not give it to you.

| What you found | What to do |
|---|---|
| Active thread, under ~30 days, same topic | **Reply into it** |
| Old thread, different topic, or 3+ months cold | **New email** — a reply into a stale thread is confusing |
| Shared group thread (an event, an intro) | **Reply into it.** The shared context is the strongest opener you have |
| Nothing anywhere | **New email.** A brief intro is allowed here, and only here |
| **A call happened since the last message, and this email delivers something from it** | **New email, subject named for the deliverable** |

That last row matters. After a call, the call is the shared context, not the old thread — and the subject line has to survive being searched for in six months. It applies **only** when the email carries a real deliverable. A post-call note with nothing in it goes back in the thread, and probably should not be sent at all.

**Never re-introduce yourself to someone who knows you.** If any thread, meeting, or prior exchange exists, "I'm [name], CEO at [company]" and every variant is banned. Open with something real instead: "Been a while since our last call." / "Good talking Friday." / "Hope things are going well at [company]."

### Stop and ask before drafting if

- There is a meeting with this person on the calendar in the next 2 weeks
- There has been any exchange in the last 7 days that you were not asked to follow up on
- There is an active deal in proposal or negotiation
- A teammate is actively managing the relationship
- The CRM company name does not match the contact's email domain — that is usually a bad record join, not a job change

Do not guess past any of these. Ask.

## 8. Never email a personal address at a large company

Email the corporate address only. Never a gmail, hotmail, or yahoo address — even when the CRM has it, even when it is the only address on file.

An owner-to-employee email arriving at a personal inbox reads as a workaround. It can put the recipient in an awkward position with their employer, it looks like the address was scraped, and at enterprise or regulated accounts it can damage a live vendor relationship.

**What counts:** enterprises, public companies, global brands, banks, pharma, big agencies and their holding groups.

**What does not:** somebody's own firm, or their own personal domain. A founder at their own agency using a gmail is fine — that is their working address. So is a personal domain they hand out themselves.

**When the only address on file is personal:** do not send, and do not guess the corporate format from a colleague's address. Get it verified, and use LinkedIn in the meantime.

## 9. Pre-send checklist

Run every line. If one fails, fix it and run the list again.

1. CRM BCC address is in the `bcc` field
2. Reply-vs-new was actually run, not assumed — including the post-call exception
3. No re-introduction if any prior relationship exists
4. Full banned-word sweep, em dashes included
5. **One** value item
6. The email delivers something — a link, a login, a document, a number, a decision
7. One line they did not know, and none of their own insight fed back to them
8. One action at the close, and it is open, not a yes/no
9. Under 150 words
10. Signature exact
11. Every claim is checkable — numbers match the source, their tools are called what they call them
12. Recipient's address is corporate if they work at a large company
13. Name spelled right, and it is the real person, not a mis-joined CRM contact
14. **Draft only.** The owner reviews and sends every email.

## What not to do

- Never send. Drafts only, always.
- Never send an email that only promises something. Build it, then send.
- Never feed someone their own insight back as if it were news.
- Never close with a yes/no or a pick-one question.
- Never put more than one ask in an email.
- Never write product claims or pricing from memory — pull them live.
- Never invent a number.
- Never use em dashes in an email body.
