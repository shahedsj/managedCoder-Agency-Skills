# Owner Blog Post

Generic leadership content is free and worthless. The only thing an agency owner can write that nobody else can is what actually happened to them — the number, the date, the thing that broke, the decision they got wrong. This skill gathers that material first, then writes it, in their voice, short.

If a paragraph could appear on any AI or leadership blog, it does not belong in this post.

> Tool placeholders like `~~meeting notes` mean whatever tool you've connected in that category. See [CONNECTORS.md](../CONNECTORS.md).

## How it works

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STANDALONE (always works)                                    │
│  ✓ Paste notes, a transcript, or just talk through what       │
│    happened. The post gets built from that.                   │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  SUPERCHARGED (when you connect your tools)                   │
│  + ~~meeting notes: real quotes and moments from transcripts  │
│  + ~~docs: past posts, to learn the voice and avoid repeats   │
│  + ~~project tracker: real dates for when things were built   │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Voice guard, applied throughout:** these are short, personal, first-person posts. No Key Takeaways box. No FAQ section. No stat-stuffing. No charts. If a section reads like a marketing blog, cut it.

## Step 1 — Gather material before drafting anything

Do not draft blind. Pull real source material on the topic first:

- **Meetings and transcripts** over the relevant window. Look for direct quotes and specific moments, not topic summaries.
- **Past posts** on the same theme. These usually contain the most honest usable lines — the doubt, the specific number, the admission.
- **Build history.** When was it actually built, renamed, relaunched? Real dates make the story credible.

Compile a short research summary before writing. Mark what is usable as-is (a real quote, a real event) versus what is only context.

**Verify or drop — never soften.** Every quote, date, and number must trace to something you found. Note the source next to each specific claim. If a claim cannot be traced, **remove it** — do not fuzz it into vague language to keep it. When in doubt, ask. Never invent a struggle, a quote, or a date.

## Step 2 — Learn the voice once, reuse it forever

Look for a voice profile file saved alongside this skill. If there isn't one, build it now from 5 to 10 of the owner's strongest past posts:

- How they open
- Their cadence and typical paragraph length
- Phrases they actually use
- Words they never use

Keep it as short prose, not metrics. Save it. Every future post starts faster and sounds more like them. Offer to update it whenever they correct a draft's voice.

## Step 3 — Confirm the angle before writing the full draft

Do not spend a full draft on the wrong moment. Show a quick skeleton first:

- The exact **hook** — the real moment, in one or two lines
- The **thesis** in one sentence
- A one-line note for each middle beat

Get a yes or a redirect, then write. Keep this short — it is a gut check on direction, not an outline document. Skip it only if they say "just write it."

## Step 4 — Draft to this shape

1. **Hook** — a real, specific personal moment. Ideally already in their own words from a past post or transcript.
2. **The honest reframe** — why they actually built it or did it. Usually more human and less strategic than the version told in public.
3. **What broke, or was harder than expected** — the real technical or organizational lesson. This is the part readers remember.
4. **The current thesis** — the one idea they want the reader to leave with.
5. **Short close** — no generic CTA. End on the point itself, or a one-line invitation to respond.

First person. Present tense where it reads naturally. Specifics over abstractions, every time.

## Step 5 — Humanize, then self-review before showing anyone

Before calling any draft final:

- No em dashes, no en dashes, no `--` used as punctuation
- No banned buzzwords (see [`12 Write in My Voice`](12-write-in-my-voice.md))
- Short paragraphs, 1 to 3 sentences
- Cut hard. A first draft usually loses 15 to 25% of its words with no loss of meaning
- Direct opening. No throat-clearing
- End on a clear point, not a vague question

**Then self-review before showing the owner.** They should not be the one to catch a basic miss. Check, and fix, silently:

1. Every specific claim still traces to a real source. Nothing crept in unverified.
2. It sounds like them, not like an advice blog.
3. No em dash, no double hyphen, no buzzword slipped through.
4. It is actually short. No section drifted into filler.
5. Every beat carries a first-hand detail only they could write — a real name, number, or moment.

Report back in 2 to 4 bullets what you changed. Not just "done."

## Step 6 — Find the right home before saving

Do not assume a new page. Check where the series already lives, find the next number, and confirm the post is not near-duplicating one that already exists. Overlap with an earlier post is worth flagging before publishing, not after.

If the topic does not fit the existing series, ask rather than inventing a new location.

## Step 7 — Always ask before saving anywhere

Never write to a doc tool, drive, or CMS without:

1. Showing the final content in full
2. Naming the exact destination
3. Getting an explicit yes

## The rules that make this work

**The specific beats the insightful.** "We deployed 70 agentic solutions across 70 companies and learned what actually sticks" is worth more than three paragraphs about the future of AI adoption. The reader can get the second one anywhere.

**Write the part that is slightly embarrassing.** The draft that survives editing is usually the one where the owner admits what they got wrong. The polished version of the same story is forgettable.

**A post that could have been written by an agency's marketing team should be cut, not improved.** There is no editing path from generic to personal.

**Short is a feature.** These posts work at 400 to 700 words. Past that, the honesty gets diluted by structure.

## Worked example

**Input:** "Write a blog about my journey building our internal AI brain."

**What Step 1 found:** a transcript line from a leadership meeting where the owner said the tool was built "so I stop being the bottleneck, not to replace anyone." A past post admitting the first version was abandoned after six weeks. Version history showing it was rebuilt twice.

**The skeleton that got approved:**
> Hook: the six weeks of work that got deleted.
> Thesis: one big model does not run a company. Orchestration does.
> Middle: built it to remove myself as the bottleneck → first version failed because every output still needed a human checkpoint and nobody had time to be the checkpoint.

**What got cut in Step 5:** a paragraph about the future of AI in professional services. True, well written, and something any consultant could have published. The rebuilt-twice detail replaced it.

## Tips

1. **Record the moment when it happens.** The honest version of a story has a shelf life of about two weeks before it becomes the polished version.
2. **The best hook is usually already written** — in a message they sent someone at the time, or a line from a meeting. Search before you write.
3. **If the post needs a statistic to be interesting, the story is not the right one.**
4. **Read the close out loud.** If it sounds like a CTA, it is one. Cut it.
5. **One post, one idea.** Two theses in one post means two posts.
